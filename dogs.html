Dog owners

Healthy activity through every stage of life

The domestic dog is an extremely social animal, with well over 300 breeds recognized by the World Canine Organization. Care needs change throughout a dog's lifetime, particularly for food, hydration, activity, grooming, handling, and preventive care.

Diet & Exercise

Puppies should be nursed the first two months, then introduced to three meals per day when they are about three to six months old. Intake can be reduced to two meals per day from six months to one year and to one meal after one year.

A dog's typical body mass is composed of somewhere between 60 and 70 percent water. The ASPCA says a 10 percent decrease in body water can cause illness and a 15 percent loss can cause death, so keep your dog hydrated.

Activity needs vary greatly by breed and size. The supplied guidance gives a general range of 30–120 minutes of exercise per day. Brisk walks count. Toys can also encourage physical activity, but should be age appropriate, free of avoidable choking hazards, and supervised when required.

Play supports more than energy

Use walks, play, appropriate toys, and fresh-air time to build healthy daily routines. Watch for signs that an activity is too intense or unsafe for your dog.

Grooming & Handling

Brush frequently to reduce shedding and prevent matted or tangled fur. Check for insects such as ticks and fleas, especially after visiting heavily wooded areas.

Bathing is important to health and can become exercise depending on how strong-willed the dog is. Rinse all soap out because residual soap can cause a rash. Use soap formulated for puppies or dogs since harsh soaps can cause allergic reactions.

When carrying a small dog, cradle the pup with one hand under the chest and the forearm supporting the back half. For a large dog, reach under the belly with both arms perpendicular to the dog, supporting the chest and rear as you lift.

Vaccinations & Medications

You should always consult with your veterinarian. A core set of vaccines is typically recommended to reduce exposure risk to diseases such as hepatitis, rabies, and parvovirus. Others may be determined by your vet based on environment and risk.

Examples may include Bordetella bronchiseptica, Borrelia burgdorferi, and Leptospira bacteria.
